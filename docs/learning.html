How the Fly Learns

The demo shows a computer model of a fruit fly's brain learning that one smell means a treat is coming. But the model was built from a wiring diagram — a map of the brain, not the brain itself. So what actually changes when it "learns"? The short answer: a small set of connections gets weaker, by the same rule real flies use. This page explains that in plain terms. The numbered notes hold the technical detail for anyone who wants it.

On a phone, tap a note number to open it.

"How is the fly learning? What's under the hood? I thought we didn't have a good idea of how biological brains learned mechanically. Is it realistic, or a hack?" — a friend, on seeing the demo. A fair question.

1 · The fly's memory organ

Deep in a fruit fly's head sit two small structures called mushroom bodies, named for their shape. This is where the fly stores what smells mean. Here is the circuit, drawn from the actual wiring diagram of a fly brain:

Read it left to right. A smell lands on a couple of thousand sensor cells in the antennae. A sorting station tidies the signal. Then it fans out across about five thousand memory cells — but a built-in damper makes sure only a few dozen of them wake up for any given smell, and a different few dozen for each smell.The memory cells are Kenyon cells (KCs). Each listens to a random handful of the 685 projection neurons leaving the antennal lobe and fires only when several agree; the two giant APL neurons feed inhibition back so only the best-matched cells win. This is sparse coding. In our runs each odor activates 1–4% of KCs, two odors' codes overlap by about 1% (Jaccard 0.01), and the same odor gives the same code every time (Jaccard 1.00). Every memory cell is wired to a handful of about a hundred decision cells, and the decision cells vote: move toward this smell, or away from it. Finally, a group of reward cells fire when something good happens to the fly, like tasting sugar.The decision cells are mushroom-body output neurons (MBONs), 96 of them in 35 types. The reward cells are dopamine neurons: 307 in the PAM cluster (reward) and 16 in PPL1 (punishment). All counts come from the model's cell-type labels (experiments/learning/mb_subnet_ids.json).

The key idea. Each smell has its own small crew of memory cells, and each memory cell has connections to the decision cells. Those connections are the knobs. Turn down the knobs on one smell's crew, and that smell's grip on the decision changes — without touching any other smell. Reward cells are the hand that turns the knobs.

2 · How do we know this is where flies learn?

It is true that for most brains, including ours, nobody knows the mechanism of learning at this level of detail. The fly mushroom body is the famous exception. Fly researchers can switch specific cells on and off with light and genetics, and record from single cells while the fly learns. Over twenty years, that toolkit produced four findings:

  • Break the mushroom body and the fly can't learn smells. Mutants and lesions localized smell-memory here long before anyone could watch it happen.Heisenberg 2003 reviews the classical mutant and lesion evidence. Aso et al. 2014a then showed the anatomy reads as an architecture for learning: the sparse KC code feeds 15 compartments, each pairing specific dopamine neurons with specific MBONs.
  • Flashing the reward cells works as well as sugar. Shine light on the reward cells while the fly smells something, and it learns to like that smell — no sugar involved.Claridge-Chang et al. 2009 ("Writing memories with light-addressable reinforcement circuitry"); Aso et al. 2014b mapped which dopamine neurons write reward vs. punishment. This optogenetic substitution is why our simulation can deliver reward directly at the dopamine neurons (§5).
  • The knobs really do turn down. Recording a decision cell before and after training shows the connections from the trained smell's memory cells get weaker. Weaker, not stronger: the fly learns by turning a dial down.Hige et al. 2015 measured this by patch-clamp: pairing an odor with dopamine-neuron activity depresses that odor's KC→MBON synapses in the matching compartment. Depression of the "wrong" output is the established sign of the change.
  • Dopamine is the trigger. The reward cells release dopamine onto exactly these connections, and the chemistry of the dopamine receptors is what detects "smell and reward at the same time."Cohn, Morantte & Ruta 2015 showed dopamine release is compartmentalized and state-dependent; Handler et al. 2019 showed the sign and timing of the plasticity follow from two dopamine receptors with different kinetics. Modi, Shuai & Turner 2020 review the whole story; Bennett et al. 2021 is one of several computational models built on this rule.

So when we say the model uses "the fly's real learning rule," this is what we mean: same place, same trigger, same direction of change.

3 · The map

In 2024, a large team finished tracing an entire fruit-fly brain from electron-microscope images: every one of its roughly 140,000 neurons and 50 million connections.FlyWire (Dorkenwald et al. 2024): 139,255 neurons and ~50M synapses from one adult female, proofread by a community. For every pair of neurons it gives the synapse count. Schlegel et al. 2024 add a cell class and type for every neuron — that is how we know which cells are sensor, memory, decision, or reward cells. The groups in the diagram are selected by those published labels, not by us. A second team turned that map into a simulation: every neuron becomes a simple "fill up, then fire" unit, and each connection is as strong as its number of synapses.Shiu et al. 2024: leaky integrate-and-fire neurons with published constants we did not change — rest/reset −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, membrane time constant 20 ms, synaptic decay 5 ms, refractory 2.2 ms, delay 1.8 ms; each synapse contributes 0.275 mV, with sign from the predicted neurotransmitter. The model reproduces known responses (sugar → proboscis extension) with no fitting. That simulation is what runs in our demo.

Here is the catch. A map shows where the roads go, not how traffic changes them. The wiring diagram contains no rule for how connections strengthen or weaken — that machinery is invisible to the microscope. So any simulation built from a map has to have its learning rule added by hand. The honest question is not "did you add a rule?" but "is it the right rule, in the right place?"Also absent from the map: receptor identities, neuromodulator release, gap junctions, and short-term synaptic dynamics. For the learning demo we cut the map to the olfactory pathway plus mushroom body — 8,991 neurons, 792k synapses. Doing so surfaced a finding: the published whole-brain model is bistable, and almost any central input tips it into a permanent ~8,400-neuron storm. Four structural edits (each biologically argued, none a parameter fit) remove the storm while leaving odor coding intact; they are listed in the appendix and audited in notes.md.

4 · The rule

The model's memory is a list of about 62,000 numbers: the strength of every connection from a memory cell to a decision cell. Nothing else in the model remembers anything from one moment to the next.62,261 KC→MBON connections (recomputed from the subnet's cell lists and the FlyWire connectivity table). Their weights start at the connectome value. Membrane voltages and synaptic conductances are reset between episodes; only this weight vector carries over.

The rule that changes those numbers fits in one sentence:

Whenever the reward cells fire, every connection coming out of a memory cell that was active at the same time gets cut in half.

That is the whole algorithm.Verbatim from learning_driver_mb.py: kc_active = KCs with ≥1 spike this episode pam_hz = mean PAM DAN rate this episode if us and pam_hz >= 1.0: # dopamine gate hot = synapses whose presynaptic KC is in kc_active w[hot] *= (1 - eta) # eta = 0.5 Dopamine-gated long-term depression at KC→MBON, applied once per episode. No objective function, no gradient, no hidden state. No training loop, no error signal, no clever optimization — just "cut these in half."

Which parts are real, and which are simplified? The three things that make this the fly's rule rather than a generic one all match what §2 established: the place (memory-to-decision connections), the trigger (reward cells firing at the same time as the smell), and the direction (weaker). Everything about the fine print is our simplification. In a real fly the change is gradual and depends on precise timing; we apply it in one step at the end of each half-second.Real plasticity is continuous and receptor-mediated, with sign and size depending on odor–dopamine timing at sub-second resolution (Handler 2019). Our episodic "same episode = coincident" abstraction cannot reproduce forward-vs-backward pairing effects. A real fly's reward cells are organized into compartments that write different lessons to different decision cells; we use one reward signal for all of them.Biology: 15 compartments, each DAN type writing to its own MBON zone, appetitive and aversive traces in parallel (Aso 2014, Cohn 2015). Model: one scalar gate (mean PAM rate ≥ 1 Hz) and one uniform η. Also: real DANs make fast synapses too; we zeroed those for stability, so dopamine acts only through the scripted rule. And real memories fade and can be unlearned; ours only ever get stronger.Depression only, permanent. No recovery, extinction, or forgetting. The full biology-vs-model ledger is in the appendix.

5 · Teaching the fly

You probably know Pavlov's dog: ring a bell before every meal, and soon the dog drools at the bell alone. Flies learn the same way with smells. Give them smell A together with sugar, smell B with nothing, and later they walk toward A.In conditioning jargon, the smell is the conditioned stimulus (CS) and the sugar the unconditioned stimulus (US). The classic fly assay is Tully & Quinn 1985: train a group with odor A + sugar or shock and odor B alone, then let them choose between A and B in a T-maze; the fraction choosing A is the memory score. B is the control — it experiences everything except the smell–reward coincidence. Our simulation does the same thing, one half-second episode at a time:

A "smell" is a particular set of sensor cells switched on for half a second. The "reward" is the reward cells switched on during that same half-second — the light-flash version of sugar from §2, not sugar itself.CS: a disjoint set of six ORN receptor classes, count-balanced, driven at 500 Hz for 0.5 s. US: the 307 PAM dopamine neurons driven at 60 Hz. We inject the US at the DANs because the model's own sugar→PAM route is silent in this regime (measured: PAMs at 0 Hz under sugar drive) — a documented limitation, but one with a real experimental precedent. We present smell A with reward five times, with smell B and no reward in between as the control. Then we test: how strongly do the decision cells respond to each smell now?

The result: the response to smell A drops by about 99%, while smell B — whose memory cells were never active during a reward — is untouched.Five pairings at η = 0.5 compound to ~97% depression per trained synapse. A's MBON response falls from 3,700–4,300 spikes to 0–57 across three seeds; B stays at 3,400–4,300. The MBON-spike readout uses a ×20 efficacy correction on KC→MBON synapses (spiking MBON responses in the stable regime are dominated by a few direct PN→MBON connections); the synaptic-trace readout needs no correction and shows the same picture. Details and sensitivity in the report. We can't watch this fly walk to a T-maze arm here — the navigation demo does that — so the decision cells' response stands in for the behavior.

One more thing, and it is the part we find most satisfying. Smells that partly resemble A also get partly suppressed — the more similar, the more suppressed. Nothing in our rule says that should happen. It happens because similar smells share some of the same memory cells, so they share some of the same turned-down knobs. That is the real wiring doing the work, not us.Probe odors sharing 67/50/33/0% of A's receptor classes inherit graded suppression (~60–75% at 67% overlap, falling to 0 at disjoint). The synaptic-trace version of this gradient needs no tuned parameters at all. This is the qualitative signature of generalization gradients in real flies.

So: realistic, or a hack? Both, and it is worth being precise about which is which. The place, the trigger, and the direction of the change are the fly's, established by the experiments in §2. The way similar smells share the memory comes from the real wiring in §3. What is ours is the fine print: one big step instead of a gradual change, one reward signal instead of many, memories that never fade. We chose the simplest rule the evidence supports and kept a full list of the simplifications. The test of "realistic" is whether the result depends on our choices; the one place it partly does — how we read out the decision cells — is flagged in the notes, and the similarity result survives with nothing tuned at all.
