How the Fly Learns

The demo shows a connectome acquiring a Pavlovian memory. But a connectome is a static wiring diagram — so what actually changes when the fly "learns"? This page answers that precisely: which synapses update, by what rule, what published biology that rule comes from, and which parts are simplifying assumptions of ours rather than facts about flies.

"How is the fly learning? What's under the hood? I thought we didn't have a good idea of how biological brains learned mechanically. How does the state actually get updated — is it realistic, or a hack?" — every sharp reader of the demo, correctly.

The short answer. The persistent state is one weight vector: the 62,261 Kenyon-cell→MBON connections in the mushroom body. Whenever an odor's Kenyon cells fire in the same half-second episode as the dopamine "reward" neurons, exactly those Kenyon cells' output synapses get multiplied by (1 − η). Nothing else changes. That rule — dopamine-gated depression at KC→MBON synapses — is not our invention: it is the experimentally established mechanism of associative learning in the fly, arguably the best-understood learning mechanism in any brain. Our implementation of it, however, is deliberately minimal, and this page keeps a complete ledger of the simplifications.

What a connectome contains — and what it doesn't

The FlyWire connectome (Dorkenwald et al. 2024) is anatomy: which neuron synapses onto which, how many synapses each connection has, and a prediction of each neuron's neurotransmitter (hence each connection's sign). Following Shiu et al. 2024, we run this as a spiking network by treating synapse count × sign as a fixed weight.

What a connectome does not contain: any learning rule. Wiring diagrams are snapshots; plasticity — the machinery that changes synaptic strength with experience — is invisible to electron microscopy. So any connectome model that learns must have a plasticity rule added by hand. The scientific question is only whether the added rule is the one biology actually uses, applied at the site biology actually uses. Here, it is — at cartoon resolution.

A second correct instinct: we genuinely do not know, at mechanistic level, how most brains learn — how cortex assigns credit is an open problem. The fly mushroom body is the famous exception. Two decades of cell-type-specific experiments (below) pinned down the site, the signal, the direction, and the behavioral consequence of the change. That is exactly why this circuit is the right first learning demo for a connectome.

The mushroom body in ninety seconds

The demo restricts the connectome to the olfactory pathway plus the mushroom body — 8,991 neurons, 792k synapses, group sizes below taken from the model itself.

The computational logic, in one pass: the antennal lobe cleans up the receptor signal; the 685→5,177 expansion plus APL inhibition turns each odor into a small, nearly non-overlapping set of active Kenyon cells (in our runs: 1–4% of KCs, overlap between two odors ≈ 1%, repeat-reliability ≈ 100%). Because the code is sparse, changing only the active cells' output synapses edits the meaning of one odor without touching others. The MBONs are the dial being edited: depress the active-KC→MBON synapses and that specific odor's grip on those MBONs weakens — which real flies read out as a changed approach/avoid bias.

The mechanism, as biology established it

This is the part that surprises people: for this one circuit, the field really does know the learning rule. The key results, each from cell-type-specific experiments in living flies:

  • The site. Memory formation localizes to the mushroom body (Heisenberg 2003 for the classical evidence), and the anatomy — sparse KC code, 15 compartments each pairing specific DANs with specific MBONs — reads as an architecture for associative learning (Aso et al. 2014a).
  • The signal. Dopamine neurons carry the reinforcement: PAM-cluster DANs signal reward, PPL1-cluster DANs punishment. Driving them optogenetically in place of real sugar or shock writes a memory (Claridge-Chang et al. 2009; Aso et al. 2014b) — which is why our injected "US" below is a legitimate move, not a cheat.
  • The change. Pairing an odor with DAN activity depresses the synapses from that odor's Kenyon cells onto the compartment's MBON — measured directly with electrophysiology (Hige et al. 2015). Depression, not potentiation: the fly learns by turning down the losing option's dial.
  • The gate. Dopamine release is compartmentalized and state-dependent (Cohn et al. 2015), and the sign and timing of plasticity follow from two dopamine receptors with different kinetics (Handler et al. 2019) — coincidence detection is implemented in receptor biochemistry.

Modi, Shuai & Turner (2020) review the whole story under the apt title "from architecture to algorithm." Computational models built on this rule are themselves an established genre (e.g. Bennett et al. 2021). So the demo's learning rule has a citation trail; what it doesn't have is biophysical resolution — next section.

What the simulation actually does

Learning happens in discrete episodes, not continuously. One episode = half a second of simulated spiking, then a bookkeeping step in plain numpy:

The entire learning algorithm, verbatim from learning_driver_mb.py (η = 0.5 in the headline runs):

kc_active_idx = g['kc'][np.flatnonzero(epi[g['kc']] >= args.kc_thresh)]
pam_hz = float(epi[g['pam']].mean() / args.epi_sec)
if us and pam_hz >= args.pam_gate_hz:          # dopamine present?
    hot = np.isin(plastic_pre, kc_active_idx)  # this odor's KCs
    wp[hot] *= (1 - args.eta)                  # depress their MBON synapses
    syn.w[plastic_pos] = wp * volt

That multiply is the whole thing. There is no gradient, no objective function, no training loop in the machine-learning sense — and no hidden state beyond the weights themselves.

The full protocol around it: two pre-tests of each odor; five pairings of odor A + PAM drive, interleaved with unpaired presentations of odor B (the control — it experiences everything except the odor–dopamine coincidence); two post-tests; then probe odors sharing 67/50/33/0% of A's receptor classes. Tests and probes deliver no US, so they induce no plasticity. Five pairings at η = 0.5 compound to ~97% depression per trained synapse — hence the ~99% collapse of A's MBON response while B is untouched.

And the generalization result is where the connectome earns its keep: nothing in the rule says probes should inherit graded suppression. That gradient falls out of the real wiring — probe odors sharing receptor classes with A recruit overlapping Kenyon cells, so they inherit exactly the depressed synapses they share. The rule is imposed; the geometry of the memory is the fly's.

The assumptions ledger

Every place our implementation is a cartoon of the biology, and why it matters:

AspectReal flyThis modelConsequence
Update dynamics Continuous, receptor-mediated; sign and magnitude depend on odor–dopamine timing at sub-second resolution (Handler 2019) One batch multiply between 0.5 s episodes; coincidence = "same episode" Timing-dependence (forward vs. backward pairing) cannot emerge here
Dopamine signal Compartmentalized: 15 MB compartments, each DAN type writing to its own MBON zone, opposite valences in parallel (Aso 2014, Cohn 2015) One scalar gate — mean PAM rate ≥ 1 Hz — and one uniform η for all KC→MBON synapses No appetitive/aversive asymmetry, no parallel memory traces
Direction of change Bidirectional: depression and recovery/potentiation; memories decay and extinguish Depression only, permanent Extinction, forgetting, and re-learning are out of scope
Reward pathway Sugar sensing recruits PAM DANs through circuitry PAMs driven directly at 60 Hz — the connectome's own sugar→PAM route is silent in this LIF regime (measured) Mirrors optogenetic conditioning (a real, published paradigm) — but the natural reward path is not demonstrated
Dopamine's fast action DANs also make fast synapses (co-transmission) DAN fast outputs zeroed (stability edit); dopamine acts only through the scripted rule Clean separation, but neuromodulation exists only where we wrote it in
Synaptic weight Conductances, receptor content, short-term dynamics Synapse count × predicted transmitter sign (Shiu 2024) Crude proxy; underlies the readout-gain caveat below
Behavioral readout MBON compartment balance → downstream motor bias MBON spike counts, with a ×20 efficacy correction on KC→MBON synapses; the synaptic-trace readout needs no correction Headline % depends on readout choice; the parameter-free trace shows the same gradient (see report)
Substrate stability Stable via neuromodulation, gap junctions, adaptation — none in the wiring diagram Four structural edits to kill a global ignition attractor in the published model (each biologically argued; audit in notes.md) The substrate needed surgery before learning was even testable — a finding about connectome models, not about flies
So: realistic or a hack? Both, cleanly separated. The site (KC→MBON), signal (dopamine coincident with the odor's KC code), and direction (depression) match the established biology point for point, and the generalization structure is inherited from real wiring rather than built in. The resolution — episodic batch updates, one scalar gate, one η, LTD-only — is a hand-coded abstraction, chosen to be the simplest rule the evidence supports. What would falsify the "realistic" claim: if the result depended on the abstraction's knobs. The one place it partly does (readout gain) is flagged above and in the report; the generalization gradient survives with no tuned parameters at all.
