How the Fly Learns

In the demo, a static wiring diagram acquires a Pavlovian memory. This page walks the mechanism in the order a skeptic needs it: the organ where learning happens, how we know that, the map that let us rebuild it, the parametrization and update rule — with each piece labeled as established biology or our simplification — and finally the conditioning experiment itself.

"How is the fly learning? What's under the hood? I thought we didn't have a good idea of how biological brains learned mechanically. How does the state actually get updated — is it realistic, or a hack?" — every sharp reader of the demo, correctly.

1 · The mushroom body

Everything below happens in one structure: the mushroom body, the fly's memory organ, plus the olfactory pathway feeding it. Here is the circuit as the connectome wires it — group sizes are the model's own.

Read it left to right. Olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) each express one receptor type; the antennal lobe pools them by type and cleans up the signal. Its 685 projection neurons then fan out onto 5,177 Kenyon cells (KCs), each of which listens to a handful of random inputs and fires only when several agree — and the two giant APL neurons feed inhibition back so that only the best-matched cells win. The result is a sparse code: each odor lights up a small, nearly unique set of KCs (in our runs 1–4% of them, with ~1% overlap between two odors and ~100% repeatability). Every KC then synapses onto a few of the 96 mushroom-body output neurons (MBONs), whose relative activity biases the fly toward or away from whatever it is smelling. Dopamine neurons (DANs) — 307 in the PAM cluster, 16 in PPL1 — carry reward and punishment into the same tissue.

The design logic: because the KC code is sparse, changing only the active cells' output synapses edits what one odor means to the MBONs without touching any other odor. The KC→MBON synapse is the dial; dopamine is the hand that turns it.

2 · How we know this is where the fly learns

A fair objection is that we do not know, mechanistically, how most brains learn — how cortex assigns credit remains open. The fly mushroom body is the well-known exception. Two decades of cell-type-specific experiments in living flies pinned down the site, the signal, the direction, and the behavioral readout of the change:

  • The site. Mutants and lesions localize olfactory memory to the mushroom body (Heisenberg 2003 reviews the classical evidence), and its anatomy — a sparse KC code feeding 15 compartments, each pairing specific DANs with specific MBONs — reads as an architecture built for associative learning (Aso et al. 2014a).
  • The signal. Dopamine neurons carry the reinforcement: PAM-cluster DANs signal reward, PPL1-cluster DANs punishment. Activating them optogenetically in place of real sugar or shock writes a memory (Claridge-Chang et al. 2009; Aso et al. 2014b). This is why our simulated reward can be delivered at the DANs directly (§5).
  • The change. Pairing an odor with DAN activity depresses the synapses from that odor's Kenyon cells onto the compartment's MBON — measured directly by patch-clamp (Hige et al. 2015). Depression, not potentiation: the fly learns by turning down a dial.
  • The gate. Dopamine release is compartmentalized and state-dependent (Cohn et al. 2015), and the sign and timing of the plasticity follow from two dopamine receptors with different kinetics (Handler et al. 2019) — the coincidence detector is receptor biochemistry.

Modi, Shuai & Turner (2020) review the full story as "from architecture to algorithm," and computational models built on this rule are an established genre (e.g. Bennett et al. 2021). So the rule we implement has a citation trail. What it lacks is biophysical resolution — §4 keeps the ledger.

3 · The map that lets us rebuild it

Three published resources turn that biology into something we can run:

  • The wiring. FlyWire (Dorkenwald et al. 2024) is an electron-microscopy reconstruction of one adult female fly brain: all 139,255 neurons and ~50 million synapses, proofread by a community. For every pair of neurons it gives the synapse count.
  • The labels. Schlegel et al. 2024 annotate every neuron with a cell class and type. That is how we know which of the 139,255 are ORNs, Kenyon cells, MBONs, or PAM dopamine neurons — the groups in the diagram are selected by those labels, not by us.
  • The dynamics. Shiu et al. 2024 turn the map into a spiking model: every neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit, every connection's weight is its synapse count times the sign of the predicted neurotransmitter. This model reproduces known sensorimotor responses (sugar → proboscis extension) with no fitting, and it is the substrate for everything here.

What the map does not contain matters just as much. A connectome is a snapshot of anatomy. It records no learning rule, no receptor identities, no neuromodulator release, no gap junctions. Plasticity — the machinery that changes synaptic strength with experience — is invisible to electron microscopy. So any connectome model that learns has a rule added by hand. The scientific question is whether the added rule is the one biology uses, at the site biology uses. Here, it is — at cartoon resolution.

For the learning demo we cut the map down to the olfactory pathway plus the mushroom body: 8,991 neurons and 792k synapses. Doing so surfaced a finding of its own — the published whole-brain model is bistable, and almost any central input tips it into a permanent ~8,400-neuron storm. Four structural edits, each biologically argued and none a parameter fit, remove the storm while leaving odor coding intact; they are listed in the ledger below and audited in notes.md.

4 · Parametrization and the update rule

The fixed part. Every neuron obeys Shiu et al.'s leaky integrate-and-fire equation with their published constants, unchanged: resting and reset potential −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, membrane time constant 20 ms, synaptic decay 5 ms, refractory period 2.2 ms, transmission delay 1.8 ms. Each connection contributes 0.275 mV per synapse, positive or negative by predicted transmitter. We tuned none of these. Odors and reward enter as Poisson spike trains injected into the named neurons.

The plastic part. Exactly one class of connection is allowed to change: the 62,261 Kenyon-cell→MBON connections. Their weights start at the connectome value and are the only quantity that persists from one episode to the next — the fly's memory is this vector. The update rule, verbatim from learning_driver_mb.py:

kc_active_idx = g['kc'][np.flatnonzero(epi[g['kc']] >= args.kc_thresh)]
pam_hz = float(epi[g['pam']].mean() / args.epi_sec)
if us and pam_hz >= args.pam_gate_hz:          # dopamine present this episode?
    hot = np.isin(plastic_pre, kc_active_idx)  # synapses from the odor's active KCs
    wp[hot] *= (1 - args.eta)                  # depress them: w ← (1 − η) · w
    syn.w[plastic_pos] = wp * volt

In words: after each half-second episode, if the PAM dopamine neurons fired, every synapse from a Kenyon cell that was active in that episode onto any MBON is multiplied by (1 − η), with η = 0.5 in the headline runs. That multiply is the whole algorithm. There is no objective function, no gradient, no training loop in the machine-learning sense, and no hidden state beyond the weights.

Accurate or simplified? The three things that make it the fly's rule and not a generic one all match §2: the site (KC→MBON), the signal (dopamine coincident with the odor's KC code), and the direction (depression). Everything about the resolution is our abstraction. The ledger:

5 · Pavlovian conditioning, and how we simulate it

Pavlov's dog heard a bell (the conditioned stimulus, CS) just before food (the unconditioned stimulus, US), and after a few pairings salivated to the bell alone. Flies do the same with odors. In the classic assay (Tully & Quinn 1985) a group of flies smells odor A while receiving sugar or shock, then odor B with nothing, and is later given a choice between A and B in a T-maze; the fraction that moves toward or away from A is the memory score. The unpaired odor B is the control: it experiences everything except the odor–reinforcement coincidence. Since Claridge-Chang et al. 2009 the US can be replaced by light-activating the dopamine neurons themselves — the fly learns just the same.

Our simulation follows that design one episode at a time:

  • CS — an odor is a disjoint set of six ORN receptor classes (chosen by seed, balanced by count) driven at 500 Hz for 0.5 s. Odor A is the one to be learned; odor B shares no receptor classes with it.
  • US — reward is the 307 PAM dopamine neurons driven at 60 Hz during the same 0.5 s, exactly as optogenetic conditioning does (and, per the ledger, because the model's own sugar→PAM route is silent).
  • Protocol — two pre-tests of A and of B; five pairings of A + US, each interleaved with an unpaired presentation of B; two post-tests; then four probe odors sharing 67/50/33/0% of A's receptor classes. Tests and probes deliver no US, so they induce no plasticity.
  • Conditioned response — we cannot watch a simulated fly walk to a T-maze arm here (the navigation demo does that), so the readout is the MBON population's spike response to the odor: the quantity that, in a real fly, sets the approach/avoid bias.

Five pairings at η = 0.5 compound to ~97% depression on each trained synapse, which is why A's MBON response collapses ~99% while B's — whose Kenyon cells were never active during dopamine — is untouched. Between episodes the membrane voltages and synaptic conductances are reset; only the KC→MBON weights carry over. When the demo says the brain "remembers," that vector is the memory.

The generalization result is where the connectome earns its keep. Nothing in the rule says probe odors should inherit graded suppression. The gradient falls out of the wiring: a probe sharing receptor classes with A recruits overlapping Kenyon cells and therefore inherits exactly the depressed synapses it shares — the same generalization gradient real flies show. The rule is imposed; the geometry of the memory is the fly's.

So: realistic or a hack? Both, cleanly separated. The site, signal, and direction of the change match the established biology of §2 point for point, and the generalization structure is inherited from the real wiring of §3 rather than built in. The resolution — episodic batch updates, one scalar gate, one η, depression only — is a hand-coded abstraction, chosen as the simplest rule the evidence supports. What would falsify the "realistic" claim is a result that depends on the abstraction's knobs; the one place that partly holds (readout gain) is flagged in the ledger, and the generalization gradient survives with no tuned parameters at all.
